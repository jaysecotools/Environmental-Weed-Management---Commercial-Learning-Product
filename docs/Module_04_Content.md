## Module introduction

Modules 1 and 3 asked you to look at the site and choose an approach. This module asks a different question: **what could go wrong because of the work itself?**

Weed control is an intervention. Even when the target weed is correctly identified and the method is well chosen, the act of control can damage native vegetation, disturb soil, affect water, disturb fauna, or impact cultural values. The purpose of this module is not to make you cautious to the point of inaction — it is to help you *recognise* the risks that matter on a natural-environment site, so you can plan around them or escalate them.

This module is about risk recognition and escalation, not risk prescription. The specific controls for a given site are decided with the site manager and, where relevant, a specialist.

---

## Learning outcomes

By the end of this module you should be able to:

1. Identify the main categories of environmental risk that weed control can create on a natural-environment site.
2. Recognise how the same activity can create different risks on different sites.
3. Distinguish a legal requirement from a recommended good-practice measure.
4. Identify stop-work triggers and recognise when a risk should be escalated before work continues.

## Core learning

### Non-target damage

The most direct risk of weed control is damage to plants that were not the target. This can happen through:

- **Direct damage** — cutting, pulling, trampling, machinery, or chemical exposure affecting native plants.
- **Indirect damage** — soil disturbance, altered light or water, or loss of shelter that native plants depended on.
- **Damage during access** — the route to the weed can be as damaging as the treatment itself.

Non-target damage is often avoidable. It is most likely when the contractor has not noticed the native vegetation, has not considered how the method will affect it, or has assumed that the target weed is the only plant that matters. This connects directly to Module 2 (non-target vegetation) and Module 3 (method selection).

### Threatened, significant or locally important species and communities

Some sites contain species or ecological communities that are significant at a local, state or national level. The contractor's role is not to identify these species — it is to recognise when their presence is *possible*, and to escalate before work proceeds.

Signs that a site may contain significant values:

- The site manager or site plan identifies them.
- The site contains unusual or high-quality habitat (for example, intact vegetation structure, hollow-bearing trees, or specific habitat features).
- The vegetation community is unfamiliar or looks different from the surrounding area.
- The site is small, isolated, or in a condition that suggests it may support something uncommon.

When these signs are present, the appropriate response is to pause and seek specialist input — not to proceed on the assumption that it will probably be fine.

### Fauna habitat and seasonal considerations

Fauna can be affected by control activity in ways that are easy to overlook:

- **Direct disturbance** — noise, vibration, machinery, or activity near nests, burrows, hollows or shelter sites.
- **Habitat removal** — the weed being controlled may itself be providing shelter or nesting material for some species, even if it is a weed at the site scale.
- **Timing** — some species are more vulnerable at particular times of year (breeding, nesting, migration, hibernation).
- **Connectivity** — removal of dense vegetation can isolate fauna from adjacent habitat.

A contractor does not need to be a fauna specialist. The point is to notice where habitat features are present, and to consider whether the timing or method could affect them.

### Waterways, wetlands and drainage

Water is one of the highest-consequence environmental risks in weed control. This is because:

- Water moves — impacts at a site can affect downstream values.
- Waterways concentrate ecological values — riparian zones are often the most sensitive parts of a natural-environment site.
- Water is difficult to contain — sediment, disturbed soil and other materials can move further than intended.

Risks to water include soil disturbance and erosion near drainage lines; sediment entering creeks, wetlands or dams; and changes to water flow through vegetation removal. This is why riparian settings require particular care in both Module 1 (site reading) and Module 3 (method selection).

### Soil disturbance, compaction and erosion

Control activity disturbs soil. Some disturbance is unavoidable, but the *type and extent* matters:

- **Compaction** — machinery and foot traffic can compact soil, affecting water infiltration and plant growth.
- **Erosion** — exposed soil on slopes, near water, or after vegetation removal can erode and move downstream.
- **Weed seed bank exposure** — disturbing soil can bring buried weed seed to the surface, leading to a new infestation.

Soil risks are often cumulative: a small disturbance may be acceptable, but the same disturbance repeated across a site can cause lasting damage.

### Exposure pathways: drift, runoff and overspray

Where chemical methods are used, there are additional exposure pathways that a contractor should be able to recognise:

- **Drift** — movement of chemical off-target through the air (affected by wind, weather, application method).
- **Runoff** — movement of chemical off-site via water (affected by rainfall, slope, drainage).
- **Overspray** — direct application to non-target areas.

Recognising that these pathways exist is part of a contractor's general environmental risk awareness. The specifics — what to apply, how, when, at what distance from water, and under what weather conditions — are determined by the product label, the relevant training and authorised advice, not by this course. If those specifics are relevant to a job, they come from the label, ChemCERT-level training, or a specialist.

### Weather and site conditions

Weather can turn an otherwise reasonable plan into a risky one. Site conditions can do the same. Factors to consider:

- **Wind** — affects drift, spray coverage, and worker safety.
- **Rain and recent rainfall** — affects runoff, soil stability, and access.
- **Heat and fire risk** — affects worker safety and, in some methods, fire behaviour.
- **Access conditions** — soft ground, flooding, or recent disturbance can make the site unsafe or unusable.
- **Seasonal considerations** — overlapping with fauna timing and plant growth stage.

There is no single weather rule that fits every site. The point is to notice which conditions could change the risk profile of the work, and to plan accordingly.

### Cultural heritage and Aboriginal cultural values

Natural-environment sites in Tasmania are part of Country, and may contain Aboriginal cultural values — including sites, landscapes, objects, and knowledge — that are significant to Tasmanian Aboriginal people and are protected under law.

The contractor's role is to:

- **Recognise** that cultural values may be present on any natural-environment site, not only those with obvious features.
- **Not disturb** cultural material, sites or landscapes if encountered — including during access, soil disturbance or vegetation removal.
- **Escalate** to the site manager and, through them, to the appropriate Aboriginal representative or authority if anything is encountered, unclear or unexpected.
- **Respect** cultural knowledge and follow the site's cultural protocols.

This module does not attempt to describe cultural values, sites or protocols in detail. Those are matters for Tasmanian Aboriginal people and for the relevant cultural heritage authority, and they require proper consultation and permission. This course describes only the contractor's duty to recognise and escalate, not the substance of the values themselves.

### Site-specific risk assessment and stop-work triggers

Every site has its own risk profile, and a risk assessment is the process of identifying it. A contractor does not need to produce a formal document for every site, but they do need to know what a site's risks are and what would make them stop.

**Stop-work triggers** are conditions that mean the work should pause until the risk is resolved. They are different from general risks, because they are the specific conditions under which proceeding would cause avoidable harm. Examples across natural-environment work:

- A significant species, cultural site or habitat feature is encountered that was not identified in the plan.
- Weather conditions make the chosen method unsafe or likely to cause off-site impact.
- The site conditions have changed since planning (for example, recent rain, flooding, new access issues).
- Something in the site plan or client direction no longer matches what is on the ground.
- The contractor is asked to do something outside their training, authorisation or confidence.

The appropriate response to a stop-work trigger is to pause, record what was seen, and escalate to the site manager before continuing.

### Legal requirements versus good practice

Some environmental protections are legally required. Others are recommended good practice. The two are not the same, and a contractor should be able to tell them apart:

- **Legal requirements** come from legislation, regulations, permits, approvals and statutory directions. They apply whether or not the site manager asks for them.
- **Good practice** is the body of methods and behaviours that professionals in the field generally follow because they reduce risk, even where no law requires them.

Knowing which is which matters because it affects what you can and cannot be asked to do. Legal requirements are non-negotiable; good practice can be adapted to the site, but a decision to depart from it should be a conscious, recorded one, not an oversight.

## Practical takeaway

Before your next job in a natural environment, prepare a short risk list. For each of the following categories, note what is present, what could go wrong, and what would make you pause:

1. **Non-target vegetation** — what is there, and how could the method affect it?
2. **Habitat features and fauna** — what could the work disturb, and when?
3. **Water and soil** — where does water go, and where could soil move?
4. **Cultural values** — could they be present here, and who should be consulted?
5. **Site conditions** — what weather or access conditions would change the risk?

If a stop-work trigger is reached, pause, record it, and escalate. A recorded pause is professional practice; an unrecorded one is a missed opportunity to prevent harm.
